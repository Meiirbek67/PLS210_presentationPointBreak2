Education and Voting Participation in Kazakhstan's 2026 Kurultai Election

Revised research design: competing hypotheses, concepts, measurement, methods and data

PointBreak · Nazarbayev University

What changed since Part 1, and why

  • Kept: the research question and the positivist positioning
  • Feedback 1: why expect a positive link when education lowered turnout in Zimbabwe (Croke et al. 2016), and Kazakhstan is rated Not Free?
    • → Two competing hypotheses (participation vs. disengagement) and a result that tells them apart
  • Feedback 2: a positive link could come from workplace mobilization, not knowledge (Frye et al. 2014)
    • → New concept: institutional voting pressure; the test is run only among unpressured respondents
  • Mechanism revised: "confidence in voting" (I can) → "voting matters" (it works)
  • Context updated: the 2026 election was the first election to the new unicameral Kurultai

Research question

To what extent is an individual's level of education associated with their likelihood of voting in the 2026 parliamentary (Kurultai) election among eligible voters in Kazakhstan?
  • Level of analysis: individual
  • Unit of analysis: survey respondents (eligible voters, citizens 18+)
  • "Associated", not "affects": a survey can show a pattern, not prove causation

Literature → theory

Education and turnout

  • Education can increase voting (Sondheimer & Green 2010)
  • Family background and genetics explain most of the link; an effect remains in the US and Denmark, none in Sweden (Dinesen et al. 2016)
  • Absolute vs. relative education (Persson 2013; Kim 2023)

Mechanism

  • Education → political knowledge (Grönlund & Milner 2006)
  • Knowledge → participation, via efficacy (Reichert 2016)

Authoritarian context

  • In electoral authoritarian Zimbabwe, education decreased participation: deliberate disengagement (Croke et al. 2016)
  • Autocrats use employers to mobilize workers; in Russia's 2011 election about a quarter of employers did so (Frye et al. 2014)
  • Kazakhstan: regular elections, rated Not Free (Freedom House 2025)

→ The direction of the education–voting link in Kazakhstan is an open question.

Theory: one start, two endings

Higher education
→
Better understanding of politics
→
Judgment of the election

Participation branch

Election seen as fair and effective: "voting matters"
→
Vote more

Disengagement branch

Election seen as unfair or ineffective: "the result is decided"
→
Vote less

The deciding factor: what educated people believe about the fairness and effectiveness of the 2026 election.

Competing hypotheses and the decisive test

  • H1 (participation): education is associated with higher voting regardless of beliefs about the election
  • H2 (disengagement): among people who see the election as unfair, education is associated with lower voting
"Fair" group"Unfair" group
If H1, educated people vote…moremore
If H2, educated people vote…moreless
H2 is supported if, among unpressured respondents who see the election as unfair, higher education is associated with a lower likelihood of voting. H1 is supported if the association stays positive.

The same test is repeated with perceived effectiveness, to see which belief drives disengagement.

Quality: voter freedom is deliberately kept out of "fairness" so it does not overlap with institutional pressure (discriminant validity).

Measurement: indicators and scales

ConceptIndicatorScale
Education level"What is the highest level of education you have completed?" Primary or less … Secondary · College / specialized secondary · Some university, no degree (incl. current students) · Bachelor's · Master's · PhD · Other (write-in) · Prefer not to sayOrdinal
Voting"Did you vote in the 2026 Kurultai election?" Yes / No / Prefer not to sayBinary
Perceived fairness3 items: equal opportunity to run · fair media access · honest counting. "Very much" (5) … "Not at all" (1), + Don't know / Prefer not to sayOrdinal items → index (average), treated as approximately interval
Perceived effectiveness3 items: the Kurultai has real power to make laws · influence government decisions · address public issues (same scale)Ordinal → index
Institutional pressure3 items (workplace or place of study): encouraged · pressured or required · asked for proof. Yes / No / Not applicable / Prefer not to sayBinary

Groups: index > 3 = "fair", < 3 = "unfair", exactly 3 excluded and reported. Group A (pressured) = "yes" to pressure or proof; encouragement alone does not count.

Quality of measurement

Validity

  • Content: one item per dimension of each definition
  • "Real power" added, so the item asks for a judgment, not a textbook fact
  • Double-barreled items split (run vs. media; encourage vs. pressure)
  • "Encouraged" item kept so mild reminders are not misread as pressure

Reliability

  • Several items per index: one misread item matters less
  • Measurement equivalence: the survey is bilingual (Kazakh and Russian); "fair" must mean the same in both versions → pilot both
  • Education is a factual, stable attribute

Expected variation: risks

  • Voting: over-reporting pushes answers toward "yes"
  • Pressure: fear pushes answers toward "no"
  • Fairness: fear pushes answers toward "fair" → the decisive "unfair" group may be small

Methods and sampling

Collection: online survey

  • RQ asks about patterns across many individuals
  • Perceptions can only be reported by respondents themselves; official records cannot show beliefs
  • Links each person's education to their own vote, beliefs and pressure

Analysis: subgroup comparison

  • Turnout by education level within group B (no pressure), "unfair" (and "ineffective") respondents
  • H1 and H2 differ only there; a pooled comparison would hide H2

Sampling

  • Convenience sample: link shared via Telegram, WhatsApp and Instagram
  • No sampling frame (no list of eligible voters) → no random sample
  • Screening question: citizens aged 18+
  • Invitation: "we want to hear from everyone, including people who did not vote"
  • → Weak external validity: results do not represent all eligible voters

Why not interviews?

  • The RQ asks whether education and voting are associated, not why; reasons are left to future research

  • Treated as a reference, not the truth: observers reported gaps between observed and official turnout (The Times of Central Asia 2026) → the survey–official gap is a minimum estimate of the problem

  • Feasibility: each split shrinks the groups; group B needs enough doubters

Threats, robustness and limitations

Four biases, all toward H1

  • Workplace pressure: doubters vote anyway
  • Hidden pressure: they report "no pressure"
  • Over-reporting of voting
  • Self-selection: disengaged doubters do not click the survey

→ A conservative test for H2: if H2 appears despite these biases, it is more credible. An H1 result needs caution.

Robustness checks

  • "Prefer not to say" on counting or pressure items: run the test twice (classified each way)
  • Same result both times → robust; different → conclusion depends on classification

Limitations

  • Association, not causation
  • Shows a pattern consistent with disengagement, not respondents' reasons
  • Convenience sample: limited generalization
  • Political knowledge (first step of mechanism) is not measured

Ethical considerations

  • Risk: questions on election fairness and workplace pressure are sensitive in a Not Free context; answers could attract unwanted attention
  • Informed consent screen: real purpose of the research, voluntary participation, privacy and confidentiality, potential risks
  • Soft wording; "Prefer not to say" on every sensitive item
  • Confidentiality, not anonymity: an online tool may record emails or IP addresses → turned off where the tool allows
  • Data storage: university drive; access limited to the researcher; identifying details (e.g., named workplaces in write-ins) removed before analysis; data deleted after the course
  • AI rule: no identifiable data (emails, IPs, write-in answers) pasted into AI tools
